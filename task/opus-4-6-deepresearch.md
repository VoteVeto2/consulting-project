# Pricing the unprofitable: A frequency-severity playbook

**The classical two-stage Poisson-Gamma GLM is the right answer for two non-linkable insurance datasets — not a compromise, but the canonical estimator that every major actuarial textbook prescribes.** With 24,774 frequency records and 12,256 severity records sharing covariates but no policy-level join key, the only feasible — and statistically defensible — workflow is to fit $P(\text{claim} \mid x)$ on the frequency file, fit $E[\text{cost} \mid \text{claim}, x]$ on the severity file, and combine them into an expected-loss score $\hat{\pi}(x) = \hat{p}(\text{claim} \mid x) \cdot \hat{\mu}(\text{cost} \mid \text{claim}, x)$. The catch: without premium, "unprofitable" is necessarily a *relative* concept benchmarked against the portfolio mean, and the independence assumption between frequency and severity must be invoked rather than tested. This brief synthesizes the actuarial literature, modern machine-learning extensions, and a practical implementation roadmap for a master's-level consulting exam, including model validation, customer segmentation, management communication, and honest disclosure of limitations.

The compound loss $S = \sum_k Y_k$ underpins all of non-life pricing: De Jong & Heller (2008), Ohlsson & Johansson (2010), Frees (2010), Denuit et al. (2007/2019), Werner & Modlin (2016) and the practitioner-standard CAS Monograph 5 (Goldburd, Khare, Tevet & Guller 2020) all describe the same architecture. The French MTPL benchmark — `freMTPL2freq` and `freMTPL2sev` — is itself distributed as two separate tables, which makes it the ideal reproducible analogue for this engagement.

## The classical decomposition and why it fits

The pure (technical, risk) premium is **$\pi(x) = \text{exposure} \cdot E[N \mid x] \cdot E[Y \mid x]$** under $N \perp Y$. When only a binary indicator $I = \mathbf{1}\{N \ge 1\}$ is observed (this engagement's situation), it becomes $\pi(x) = P(\text{claim} \mid x) \cdot E[\text{total cost} \mid \text{claim}, x]$ — the **two-part / hurdle decomposition** of Frees (2010, Ch. 16) and Mullahy (1986).

**Frequency side.** A Poisson GLM with log link and exposure offset, $\log(\lambda_i d_i) = \log(d_i) + x_i^\top\beta$, is the workhorse; coefficients $\exp(\beta_j)$ are interpreted as **rate ratios**. If only a claim/no-claim indicator exists, a logistic GLM gives odds ratios; with variable exposure the **complementary log-log link** is theoretically preferable because it is consistent with an underlying Poisson process ($P(N \ge 1) = 1 - \exp(-\lambda d)$) (Ohlsson & Johansson 2010 §2.3; Goldburd et al. 2020 §3.1.1). Insurance counts almost always exhibit overdispersion — $\mathrm{Var}(N) > E(N)$ — so a **Negative Binomial** GLM (`MASS::glm.nb`) or quasi-Poisson with dispersion $\hat{\phi}$ is the standard remedy (Denuit et al. 2007, Ch. 1–3).

**Severity side.** The default is a **Gamma GLM with log link** because it gives constant coefficient of variation and a multiplicative rating plan that meshes with the frequency model. **Lognormal regression** (OLS on $\log Y$) handles heavier tails but requires a $\sigma^2/2$ retransformation correction that is widely forgotten. **Inverse Gaussian** is reserved for very heavy tails. Standard practice caps large losses at a threshold (e.g., €100,000 in the scikit-learn freMTPL2 tutorial) and models the excess separately via extreme-value theory (De Jong & Heller 2008 Ch. 9; Klugman, Panjer & Willmot 2019 Ch. 5–6).

**The independence caveat.** The product form $\pi = P(\text{claim}) \cdot E[Y \mid \text{claim}]$ requires $Y \perp N \mid x$. Empirically this fails in some lines: **Garrido, Genest & Schulz (2016, IME 70: 205–215)** show negative dependence in collision motor (high-frequency drivers tend to have *minor* accidents) and positive dependence in liability. Czado et al. (2012, Scand. Actuar. J.) and Shi, Feng & Ivantsova (2015) develop copula-based corrections. **Crucially, every dependence-correction method requires record-level linkage** — Tweedie GLMs, copula models, Heckman selection, dependent-frequency-severity GLMs all assume per-policy $(N_i, Y_i, x_i)$ triples. Without IDs, none can be estimated, which is the formal reason the marginal product is not just acceptable but *the only identifiable option*. Garrido et al. (2016, Fig. 1–2) reassure that for moderate dependence the bias on the *mean* aggregate loss is typically a few percent; the larger bias is in **variance and tails**, which matters for capital and reserving but less for relative customer ranking.

## Defining "unprofitable" without premium

Werner & Modlin's *Basic Ratemaking* (2016, Ch. 8) defines pure premium as "average loss per exposure … equivalent to the product of frequency and severity." This is the only data-driven proxy for the actuarially fair price when commercial premium is absent. **Profitability becomes portfolio-relative**: classify customer $i$ as unprofitable when the loss factor $LF_i = \hat{\pi}_i / \bar{\mu}_{\text{portfolio}}$ exceeds a chosen threshold. Common operational rules — defensible against Goldburd et al. (2020 §7) and CAS E-Forum applications — use **decile cutoffs** (top 10%: candidate for non-renewal; deciles 8–9: re-price; deciles 6–7: monitor; lower deciles: retain) **and** multiplicative thresholds ($LF > 1.5\times$ → action). Reporting both schemes and showing they agree is best practice.

The standard loss ratio ($\text{Incurred Losses} / \text{Earned Premium}$) is **not computable without premium** — this is a primary methodological constraint that must be stated explicitly. Defensible substitutes are the relative pure-premium ratio $LF_i$, the predicted-loss decile rank (Frees, Meyers & Cummings 2011, JASA), and deviation-from-base-rate $r_i - 1$ (Werner & Modlin Ch. 9–10's "indicated relativity"). These are **risk-relativity surrogates, not loss ratios**, and the report should say so.

A specific terminology note: "**Customers At Premium Action (CAPA)**" does not appear in CAS, ASTIN, SSRN, or the major actuarial textbooks reviewed (Werner & Modlin, Ohlsson & Johansson, De Jong & Heller, Goldburd et al., Frees, Denuit et al.). It should be defined *locally* in the report — for example: *"the subset of policies whose expected-loss score $\hat{\pi}$ exceeds a chosen decile/ratio threshold and which therefore become candidates for a defined premium action — re-pricing, coverage restriction, non-renewal, or monitoring"* — and presented as project vocabulary, not as established actuarial terminology.

## Modern alternatives and why most are unavailable here

The **Tweedie compound Poisson GLM** (Jørgensen 1987; Jørgensen & de Souza 1994; Smyth & Jørgensen 2002, ASTIN 32) collapses both stages into a single model with $V(\mu) = \mu^p$, $1 < p < 2$, directly modelling pure premium per exposure unit. It is implemented in R (`tweedie`, `cplm`, `HDtweedie`, `statmod`), Python (`glum`, `statsmodels`, `sklearn.TweedieRegressor`), and as a boosted variant **TDboost** (Yang, Qian & Zou 2018, JBES). **It is structurally inapplicable to this engagement** because it requires per-policy total loss $Y_i = \sum_k Z_k$ on the same row as $x_i$. State this explicitly; do not pretend it is an option.

**Machine-learning approaches do work on each file separately.** The benchmark study **Henckaerts, Côté, Antonio & Verbelen (2021, NAAJ 25(2): 255–285)** fits Poisson regression trees, Poisson random forests, and Gamma/Poisson GBMs *separately for frequency and severity* on Belgian MTPL data — exactly the workflow this engagement should adopt as a secondary model. XGBoost (`objective="count:poisson"`, `"reg:gamma"`) and LightGBM (`objective="poisson"|"gamma"`) ingest exposure as offset/weight and respect distribution-aware loss. Modern interpretable neural-network actuarial models — **CANN** (Schelldorfer & Wüthrich 2019; "Yes we CANN!", ASTIN 49) which nests a GLM inside an MLP via skip connection, and **LocalGLMnet** (Richman & Wüthrich 2023, Scand. Actuar. J.) which outputs feature-specific $\beta_j(x)$ coefficients — also operate per-response and are summarized in the open-access reference Wüthrich & Merz (2023) *Statistical Foundations of Actuarial Learning* (Springer). With only ~12k severity rows, however, tree depth and network size must be modest and cross-validated regularization is essential.

**Zero-inflated / hurdle / Heckman models.** ZIP and ZINB (Lambert 1992) target excess zeros in counts; they are fittable on the frequency file alone (`pscl::zeroinfl`, `glmmTMB`). The classical hurdle model (Mullahy 1986) is **structurally identical to this engagement's frequency $\times$ severity decomposition**. Heckman (1979) selection requires record-linkage to identify the correlation $\rho$ between selection and outcome and is therefore unavailable here — flag this and assume independence.

**Bayesian GLMs** (`brms`, `rstanarm`, PyMC) work on each file separately, deliver posterior predictive distributions for $\hat{\pi}$ that are useful for capital and reinsurance discussions, and naturally embed Bühlmann-Straub credibility (Frees & Valdez 2008, JASA 103, Halmstad Prize) for sparse cells. They inherit the same independence assumption.

The methodological hierarchy for this engagement is therefore:

| Method | Linked data needed? | Use here? |
|---|---|---|
| Two-stage Poisson + Gamma GLM | No | **Primary baseline** |
| Tweedie GLM / TDboost / Heckman / dependent-frequency-severity copulas | Yes | Inapplicable |
| ZIP/ZINB on frequency file | No | Optional if zero excess |
| GBM (XGBoost/LightGBM, Poisson + Gamma, separately) | No | **Strong secondary model** |
| CANN, LocalGLMnet (per response) | No | Optional if volume justifies |
| Bayesian hierarchical GLM | No | Useful for uncertainty quantification |
| Single CART tree (Poisson/Gamma) | No | Diagnostic / segmentation only |

## A practical modeling pipeline

**Variable selection and collinearity diagnostics.** Compute Pearson/Spearman correlations and VIF on dummy-encoded design matrix (`car::vif`); flag $\mathrm{VIF} > 5$, drop at $> 10$. For categorical pairs use **Cramér's V** via `vcd::assocstats` ($V > 0.25$ = strong association). Belsley **condition indices** $> 30$ with two variance-decomposition proportions $> 0.5$ confirm collinearity clusters. Treat categorical levels with reference coding using the largest-volume level as base; **fuse rare levels** ($< 1\%$-$2\%$ exposure) into "Other" — Henckaerts et al. (2018, Scand. Actuar. J.) provide a data-driven binning algorithm via GAM-smooth → CART → fused levels (`distRforest`). For selection, prefer **regularized GLMs** to stepwise: `glmnet(family="poisson", offset=log(exposure))` for frequency, `glmnet(family=Gamma(link="log"))` for severity, with grouped lasso (`gglasso`) so all dummies of a categorical variable enter or exit together; tune $\lambda$ via `cv.glmnet` and the 1-SE rule. Variables surviving stepwise AIC/BIC, LASSO and elastic net are "core."

**Validation.** Report jointly: AIC, BIC, residual deviance $D$, Pearson $\chi^2$, and dispersion $\hat{\phi}$. The rule of thumb $D/df \approx 1$ for Poisson is the first overdispersion screen. The single most important diagnostic plot for non-Gaussian GLMs is the **randomized quantile residual** (Dunn & Smyth 1996, JCGS 5(3): 236–244): $r_{q,i} = \Phi^{-1}(\hat{F}(y_i))$ with uniform randomization for discrete CDF gaps; under correct specification $r_q \sim N(0,1)$. Use `statmod::qresiduals` (functions `qres.pois`, `qres.gamma`, `qres.nbinom`, `qres.tweedie`) or `DHARMa::simulateResiduals`. For out-of-sample, use 10-fold CV (`rsample::vfold_cv`) stratified on claim indicator for severity, with Poisson and Gamma deviance as loss metrics.

**Insurance-specific lift metrics.** Goldburd et al. (2020 §7) and Frees, Meyers & Cummings (2011, JASA 106: 1085–1098; 2014, J. Risk & Insurance 81(2)) define the standard set: **lift / quantile chart** (sort holdout by $\hat{\pi}$, bin into ten equal-exposure deciles, plot mean $\hat{\pi}$ vs mean $y$); **double-lift chart** comparing two models by ratio bin; **ordered Lorenz curve** with **Gini index** $= 2\times$ area between curve and $45^\circ$ line, where higher Gini means better risk discrimination. For the binary frequency component add AUC-ROC, Brier score, and a calibration plot via `rms::val.prob`. Check the **balance property** $\sum \hat{\pi} = \sum y$ on train and per major segment — canonical-link Poisson MLE satisfies it automatically; failure means the intercept needs recalibration.

**Combining into an expected-loss score.** Compute $\hat{\pi}_i = \hat{p}_i \cdot \hat{\mu}_i$ for every policy in the frequency dataset (the severity model is a per-$x$ function applied to all profiles, not just claimants). Rank, group into deciles, compute $LF_i = \hat{\pi}_i / \operatorname{mean}(\hat{\pi})$, and produce a decile table:

| Decile | n | Mean $\hat{\pi}$ | Mean observed loss | $LF$ | Cum % expected loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (lowest) | $\approx 2{,}477$ | 45 | 38 | 0.18 | 1.8% |
| 5 | $\approx 2{,}477$ | 210 | 215 | 0.84 | 39% |
| 10 (highest) | $\approx 2{,}477$ | 1,420 | 1,510 | 5.65 | 100% |

The headline "**top 10% of customers contribute X% of expected losses**" is the executive summary line. Uncertainty bands come from the delta method — $\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\pi}) \approx \hat{p}^{2} \cdot \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu}) + \hat{\mu}^{2} \cdot \mathrm{Var}(\hat{p})$ — or, more robustly, a stratified bootstrap ($B = 1{,}000$) refitting both GLMs on each replicate and recomputing $\hat{\pi}$ and $LF$.

## Customer profiling and segmentation

Three complementary segmentation lenses serve different audiences:

1. **Quantile-based scoring** is regulator-friendly and trivial to communicate — the $LF$ deciles above directly become action tiers.
2. **Mixed-data clustering** on $(\hat{\pi}, \text{profile features})$ reveals natural customer types. Use `clustMixType::kproto` (k-prototypes, Huang's $\gamma$ controlling numeric/categorical balance) or `cluster::pam` with Gower distance for robust mixed-data clustering — Yin, Gan & Valdez (2021, Risks 9: 47) document insurance applications. Validate via silhouette, between-cluster mean $\hat{\pi}$ separation, and stability under subsampling (`fpc::clusterboot`).
3. **Surrogate decision tree on $\hat{\pi}$ as response** (`rpart` with `method="anova"`, depth $\le 4$) extracts interpretable IF-THEN profiles such as "$\text{vehicle\_age} > 10$ AND $\text{region} = \text{urban}$ AND $\text{driver\_age} < 25$ $\rightarrow$ mean $\hat{\pi} = 1{,}820$, $LF = 7.2$." Henckaerts et al. (2021 §4) demonstrate this surrogate-tree pattern as the bridge between black-box ML and management presentation.

Pair these with **partial dependence plots** (`pdp::partial`), **accumulated local effects** (Apley & Zhu 2020, JRSS-B; `iml::FeatureEffect`), **permutation importance** (`vip::vi`), and **SHAP** values for individual-policy explanations.

## Communicating to management

Effective executive packs use a small, repeatable visual vocabulary. A heatmap of mean $\hat{\pi}$ across the two most important covariates (e.g., age band $\times$ vehicle type) annotated with exposure counts conveys risk concentration at a glance. A decile bar chart of $LF$ with red bars highlighting $LF > 1.5$ communicates the action segment. The lift / Gini chart with annotated Gini value plus a one-sentence caption ("the model concentrates 35% of expected losses in the top decile") quantifies discrimination power. **Profile cards** — one page per unprofitable cluster summarizing demographics, mean $\hat{\pi}$, frequency, severity, $LF$, exposure share, and recommended action — humanize the segments. Finally, an **action matrix** maps decile to recommendation:

| Decile | $LF$ range | Recommended action |
|---|---|---|
| 1–5 | $< 1.0$ | Retain; cross-sell |
| 6–7 | $1.0$-$1.5$ | Monitor; standard renewal |
| 8 | $1.5$-$2.5$ | Re-price (+10–25%) |
| 9 | $2.5$-$4.0$ | Coverage restriction or higher deductible |
| 10 | $> 4.0$ | Non-renew or refer to underwriting |

Thresholds should be tuned to retention and loss-ratio targets and **must pass anti-discrimination review** before any non-renewal rule — Lindholm et al. (2022) on discrimination-free pricing and the local rate-filing regime are the relevant references. Werner & Modlin (Ch. 6, 11) and Goldburd §8 cover stakeholder management explicitly.

## Implementation stack

The R ecosystem covers the full pipeline natively. Core GLMs use `stats::glm` (Poisson, Gamma, binomial) and `MASS::glm.nb`; penalized variants use `glmnet`, `gglasso`, and `ncvreg`; Tweedie uses `statmod::tweedie`, `cplm::cpglm`, and `HDtweedie` (as a sanity benchmark only, since linkage is absent); diagnostics use `statmod::qresiduals`, `DHARMa`, and `performance`; ML benchmarks use `xgboost` (objectives `count:poisson`, `reg:gamma`, `reg:tweedie`), `gbm`, `lightgbm`, and Henckaerts' `distRforest`; trees use `rpart` and `partykit::ctree`; workflow orchestration uses `tidymodels` (`recipes`, `parsnip`, `workflows`, `tune`, `rsample`); insurance datasets via `CASdatasets` (the canonical `freMTPL2freq`/`freMTPL2sev` mirroring this exact two-table setup); clustering via `cluster::pam`, `clustMixType::kproto`, and `factoextra`; interpretability via `vip`, `pdp`, `iml`, and `DALEX`; lift/Gini via `MLmetrics::NormalizedGini` and `ineq::Lc`. Python equivalents are `statsmodels`, **`glum`** (high-performance Poisson/Gamma/Tweedie GLM with elastic net, used by major European insurers), `scikit-learn` (PoissonRegressor, GammaRegressor, TweedieRegressor and `mean_*_deviance` metrics), `xgboost`, `lightgbm`, `pymc`, and `kmodes.kprototypes`.

The single most useful reproducible benchmark is the official scikit-learn tutorial *"Tweedie regression on insurance claims"* on freMTPL2, which fits Poisson frequency, Gamma severity (conditional on $N \ge 1$), and a Tweedie model in parallel and reports the full validation suite. The Lorentzen & Mayer GitHub repository `Tutorial_freMTPL2` and Henckaerts' tutorial notebooks (katrienantonio.github.io) extend this to GBMs and binning, and Wüthrich & Buser's ETH lecture notes (SSRN 2870308) provide the full theoretical companion.

## Honest caveats and conclusions

The two-stage marginal Poisson-Gamma GLM is **not a workaround forced by data limitations** — it is the textbook-standard pricing pipeline that every major actuarial reference describes (Ohlsson & Johansson explicitly: "the standard GLM tariff analysis is to do separate analyses for claim frequency and claim severity, and then relativities for the pure premium are found by multiplying the results"). The independence assumption is real and should be acknowledged: Garrido et al. (2016) document modest mean-prediction bias and larger tail/variance bias under dependence, and the dependence cannot be tested here because the datasets are non-linkable. With ~12k severity records and ten covariates, watch for rare-cell instability in interactions; report bootstrap confidence intervals on cluster-level $LF$ before recommending any non-renewal. Profitability is **portfolio-relative**, not absolute — if the portfolio itself is unprofitable in aggregate, "below-mean" customers may still lose money. The "CAPA" label, if used, must be defined locally; it is not established actuarial terminology. Finally, the recommended deliverable for a master's-level consulting exam is the **two-stage GLM as primary model, a Poisson/Gamma GBM as secondary benchmark, validation via deviance + randomized quantile residuals + Gini and double-lift charts, segmentation via decile loss factors plus a surrogate tree, and an action matrix accompanied by a profile-card pack** — a workflow that is fully implementable in R or Python on the freMTPL2 analogue dataset and that aligns with both the academic literature and CAS practitioner guidance.

The novel insight worth ending on: the structure of this client's data — two non-linkable tables sharing covariates — is not a defective version of an idealized linked dataset. It is the **default distribution format of the canonical academic benchmark** (French MTPL is published as `freMTPL2freq` and `freMTPL2sev`, and many published analyses simply ignore the available join key). The marginal-product approach is therefore the textbook approach, and the report's narrative should be confident: the methodology is standard, the limitations are well-understood, and the deliverable — relative ranking of customer profiles by expected loss for renewal action — is exactly what this architecture is designed to produce.
